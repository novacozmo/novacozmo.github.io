N-Body Simulations

The N-Body problem is a general problem where particles are affected by every other particle, with forces that depend on their positions, the problem being that there is no analytical solution. In astrophysics, the most common application of N-Body is gravity.

  1. N-Body Sim

    Python · Barnes–Hut Algorithm · Velocity Verlet

    My first version of an implementation of the Barnes-Hut algorithm, an N-Body algorithm that speeds up computation time from scaling by N^2 to N*log(N). Using a simple Verlet integrator to keep stability and accuracy cheaply. Made in pure python and not very optimized, more of a proof of concept, able to run a couple thousand particles at reasonable frame rates.

Dev Story · N-Body Simulations

How I Broke (and Fixed) a 2-Million-Particle Galaxy Sim

I started making N Body sims after watching Deadlock's video on the Barnes Hut Algorithm. I started with pure python making an object-based tree that used recursive division to be created. It was incredibly slow and had many artifacts due to the high theta I had to use to get any reasonable performance.

Then, after discovering Taichi, I decided to take a more parallel approach. A lot of the later work on the sim was created in my AP Computer Science Principles class for the AP performance task. I started by making a binary radix tree as that’s friendly to both storage and speed. Binary radix trees can be constructed completely in parallel and always have exactly 2N-1 nodes which was very nice since Taichi dynamically sized fields are extremely slow. At first when I realized I needed sorted Morton codes I tried making my very own radix sort, but upon failing multiple times I ended up using Taichi’s parallel_sort().

From there it was relatively easy to compute forces. After creating the physics, I then created a density based visualizer. I did this by adding a number to the pixel (and the surrounding pixels) a particle occupies then applying a function to get the brightness and color. At the time I was quite happy with it running 100,000 particles at roughly 20fps. This was around the time I submitted it to the AP Performance Task.

I then took a break from N-body to work on RingSimV2 for my AP CSP Game Jam. Only recently, in late July, I returned unsatisfied by the performance. The motivation for this was mostly due to Deadlock’s video where they made a simulation that ran with millions of particles. I ran some tests and added Taichi’s built in function profiler and found that the sort I was using to sort the Morton codes was taking hundreds of milliseconds to sort at higher N. When I looked into it, I saw that the sort I was using used a modified even-odd sort with 190 serial stages per sort. This was extremely slow, so I returned to making a radix sort. When I ran some tests on it, it showed ~26ms sort time for N=1,000,000! I plugged it into the sim (and after a couple hiccups), it ran like a charm. I was now running 500,000 particles at a very acceptable framerate.

But with how well my program scaled I thought that I could go higher. But upon trying N=1,000,000, it seemed gravity just turned off. There were no errors, no NaNs I could see, and no problems I could find with my force or tree functions. So, I let it be for a while.

When I showed my family my new snazzy simulation, my sister asked how much I could do. When I told her something broke at 1,000,000, she suggested we find exactly what N it would break. We did a bit of a manual binary search and found that at exactly N=809,551 the sim broke.

Newly motivated to solve this, I did a lot of digging and I found a NaN in the root node’s mass, and upon looking at its children, found that one of them contained a NaN’d position that broke the tree creation. After a lot of searching, I figured out what had happened. Taichi’s random() function is deterministic, and since I used that for initializing positions, any given particle would be the exact same position every time you ran a sim with the same parameters. Turns out at exactly N=809,551 the random() function gives a 0 for both x and y in the positions. A faulty While loop check allowed this to pass through and suddenly the particle was 0 units from the center. When it tried to find the velocity, it divides by r and dividing by 0 gave a NaN. This then infected the tree and when a particle tried to see if a node was close enough, nothing ever returned True because the node Center Of Mass (COM) positions were always a NaN. So, in effect, it turned gravity off.

Another bug then presented itself when I updated the initializer to give galaxies their own speeds. Their cores were being nailed to a spot! When I looked into it further, I realized the mistake was that I did a position half step for the Verlet integration after the tree was built. This allowed particles and even galaxy cores to gravitate towards their past selves, and with a dense enough region its past self’s gravity overpowered the gravity of anything else. All it took was moving the first position half step to before tree creation and galaxies could move.

After all that work, I broke the 1,000,000 particle barrier. It runs ~15fps with N=2,000,000 and the frame time scales roughly O(nlogn) as expected [see graph]. I’ve learned a lot from this program including how to make parallel functions and algorithms, work with the GPU, and even some behaviors of 2d gravity simulations. But I think, at least for now, I have finished this program.

Dev Story · Planetary Ring Simulations

The Ring Sim Story

I started making RingSimV1 a bit after my first version of my N-Body sim. I used NumPy to compute the major Body-particle forces quickly, and I was satisfied with its speed. I added features that I felt like adding, and it started to turn into this little sandbox game. I started with adding bodies you can create and throw at the ring system. I then added randomized generation for rings and moons.

A while later, after discovering Taichi, I realized there’s a lot more to ring systems. Particle collisions, and even the tiny force of gravity between ring particles, affected entire ring systems. The timescales real rings evolved at was also a problem. Verlet integration isn’t very stable with high delta time. For this new project for more accurate rings, I used a Wisdom Holman integrator.

In essence, WH integration finds the 2-body orbit of a particle around the central body (like the sun, or in this case Saturn) and moves it analytically along that orbit. This makes WH integration extremely stable with very large timesteps. That said, doing this integration efficiently is… a pain to say the least. This was due to the fact that you need to get the orbit of every particle from just its position and velocity.

I then used statistical collisions in radial/azimuth bins. These bins basically divide space into rings and then divide those rings into segments. Then, using the density, I would do statistical collisions. The collisions didn’t work very well, but the visible resonances you would see forming over long durations were satisfying.

I took a long break from rings to work on my parallel Barnes Hut N-Body sim after this, but I came back for the game jam in my AP Computer Science Principles class.

The theme was “the sun, the moon, the stars,” so it was a perfect fit. I remade my RingSimV1 with Taichi, making it able to handle tens of millions of particles without breaking a sweat. I was just a little too late to add the velocity-based color visualizer which really made the visuals so much better, but it still got the “most ambitious” award from my teacher, so I was happy.

I then added a version with collisions and N-Body, patching in the Barnes Hut N-Body sim and reusing the quadtree to find collision candidates. It worked okay, but it was pretty slow and the clumps of particles were a bit awkward. Since then, I've only really worked on optimizing and adding more features to my Barnes Hut N-Body sim, but maybe I'll come back to this and make an even more accurate simulator.
